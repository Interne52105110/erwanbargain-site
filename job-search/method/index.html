Method

Which indicators, why these, and what each one misses.

01

Indicators chosen before looking at results

The indicator list comes from the labour-economics literature, not from what makes a striking ranking.

02

A dashboard, not a single score

No composite index hides the trade-offs. Where indicators disagree, both are shown.

03

Every figure dated, sourced and graded

Official figure, estimate or own computation: the status is shown, with the formula when we computed it.

04

Open labour markets flagged

Where commuters or outside applicants make up much of the competition, resident-based ratios are marked unreliable.

Which indicators, in what order

Research on job search treats the speed at which unemployed people find work as the direct measure of difficulty. Everything else is a proxy, more or less distant. The order below is this study’s reading of that literature, not a published standard.

# Indicator What it measures What it misses In this study
1 Job-finding rate The share of unemployed people who find work in a given month. The most direct measure of what a jobseeker faces. Not published by region and profile. National estimates are computed from unemployment by duration; regional ones need survey microdata. In the report
2 Long-term share of unemployment The share of the unemployed searching for a year or more. The best published proxy for slow exits. Rises with a delay after a downturn, and depends on who becomes unemployed, not only on how fast they leave. On this site
3 Unemployment rate by age and education The only indicator published for every territory by profile. It combines the risk of losing a job and the difficulty of finding one. A poor proxy for job finding on its own: gaps between local markets come largely from job loss, not from slower hiring. On this site
4 Broad labour underutilisation Adds involuntary part-timers and people who want work but are not counted as searching. The European and Canadian measures are built differently and cannot be compared in level. On this site
5 Gap for newcomers Employment and unemployment of the foreign-born against natives, over-qualification, and correspondence tests on hiring. No study isolates French speakers moving between French-speaking countries. In the report
6 Unemployed per vacancy How many unemployed residents there are for each open position. Vacancy definitions differ by country, employed jobseekers are ignored, and the ratio breaks down wherever commuters and outside applicants compete. Context only
7 Recruitment difficulties reported by employers Surveys and shortage lists: where employers say they struggle to hire. Each country has its own survey; levels are not comparable, and a shortage for employers is not an easy hire for every applicant. Context only
8 Applicants per online ad The counter shown on job platforms. For ads that send applicants to an external site, LinkedIn counts clicks on “apply”, not completed applications. Coverage varies by occupation and town size. Not used

Reading the figures

  • Unemployment is measured at the place of residence. In Luxembourg, Geneva, Brussels and Île-de-France, a large share of jobs goes to people who live elsewhere, so resident figures understate the competition.
  • Survey rates and administrative counts are different things. Registered unemployment (France Travail, SECO, ADEM, Forem, Actiris) follows national rules and cannot be compared across countries.
  • All French figures from Eurostat for 2025 carry a “definition differs” flag. They are shown as published.
  • In Canada the education groups do not match the European ones exactly. “Higher education” covers any postsecondary credential, including trades and college.
  • The 25–54 band is not published by region and education in Europe. It is rebuilt here from the 25–34, 35–44 and 45–54 cells and marked as computed.
  • Occupation often matters more than age or diploma level. Official outlook tools score prospects by occupation; this study does not yet.
  • For Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut, no survey publishes unemployment by education. The profile shown there is modelled: the 2021 Census pattern by age and education, shifted by one constant per territory so that the overall rate matches the 2025 survey rate. Cells resting on fewer than 250 people are left out. Whether the pattern has stayed the same since 2021 has not been tested.
  • The two reports and the data of this site went through an independent review in November 2026. About 45 headline figures were confirmed against the original sources, every computed table was recomputed, and 12 errors were found and corrected. The review did not cover everything: the parts it left out are listed in the review note.
